
**Geometric Design***
- Design speed (i.e., whether to follow the [[85th Percentile Rule]] or to set targeted speeds that should not be increased)
- Sight distance - the length of road ahead that can be seen by the driver on a roadway (with shorter sight distances discouraging speeding)
- - Vertical Alignment such as road crests, sagittal curves (i.e., vertical transitions), speed bumps, and maximum or minimum grades (i.e., slopes)
- Superelevation (banking) that does not only prioritize driver comfort - https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/17098/004.cfm
- Traffic flow - the amount of cars expected to travel through a given road at certain speed, usually predicated on the [[85th Percentile Rule]] Traffic follow, in car-centric areas, typically prioritizes speed
- Level of service, defined as functional traffic conditions focused on drivers such as how much freedom drives are given to maneuver, their expected travel time, speed, comfort, and safety factors, like rumble strips or even ways to address potential interruptions when driving.
- Traffic volume - The average density of traffic that will occupy a road for an hour everyday.
- Cross Sectional Elements - Lane widths, shoulder widths, medians, cross-slopes (for water run offs), types of curves, and clear zones.

**Traffic and Operational Variables**
Required capacity and functional class of the road
- **Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT)** - A metric that measures how many trips, in both directions, a road or stretch of high way receives from all vehicles and divides those trips by a year (365) days to reach an average. Ultimately, it is an average of the yearly volume of vehicle traffic for a given road. The AADT serves as a corner stone for several metrics in traffic engineering, including:
	-  Traffic volume forecasting
	-  Tracking traffic congestion
	-  An estimate for the safety of a road
	-  An empirical measure that can be used to allocate funding for roads and highways (i.e., roads with higher AADTS receive more funding and vice versa)
	- An estimate for the value of land around a road, especially for retailers, housing developers, and commercial real-estate entities to evaluate sites of development with marketing and pricing arrangements. <span style = "color:#008B8B;">Question: How can AADTS be correlated with health? if possible, is there a way of assessing health metrics, microbiota, or other genetic forms of data from areas of high AADTS, especially with certain disease, like reparatory illnesses? </span> AADT, however, is a yearly measure, not accounting for variations in traffic volume affected by seasons, holidays, or variations in traffic patterns. 
	- **Peak Hour Volume (PHV)** - Within a time frame of four 15-minute consecutive intervals (one hour), the highest number of vehicles to occupy that road within that hourly time frame. It is a tool that is used when handling rush hour and to identify peak times (morning and evening) when rush hours contain their highest traffic volumes. It's important to understand that roads with low PHVs do not equate to not being busy, especially with differences fluctuating between different times of days (like morning vs evening commutes), lunch hours, and difference between weekday vs weekend patterns. There are also times of days were rush hour can still occur on a road that has low PHV.
	- **Design Hour Volume (DHV)** - Utilizes PHV for road design, specifically for the volume (capacity) a road is expected to handle based on the number of vehicles used to traverse the road within a given time frame, like 10000 per day as an average (although in reality, as based on the design of the road and the high traffic it receives, the DHV can be lower, such as 1000 during rush hour) <span style = "color:#008B8B;">Potential Data - Urban SDK Traffic Volume Data </span>
	  
	  **PHV and DHV influence road design factors**, including
		- Traffic signals - how signaling patterns change and are planned depending on morning, evening, and overnight/early morning hours.
		- Ramp Metering and HOV lanes - Ramp Meters are associated with roads that join arterials for entrances into freeways. But, rather than the ramp (entrance) free and open, it is instead regulated by a traffic signal. Ramp meters are used due to differences in traffic volume at certain times and are typically active only during peak rush hour(s); HOV (High Occupancy Vehicle) (colloquially called a carpool lane) is a lane, usually on a highway, reserved for carpools, vanpools, and buses - specifically cars with more than one person (though some HOV lanes require three or more people). HOV lanes are used to help quell traffic by allowing vehicles with more occupants to bypass it. Most HOV lanes usually are in effect at certain times of the day, typically workweeks during morning and evening commutes.
		- Traffic forecasting, driver suggestions, and demand management. - Tied to traffic forecasting, direct messages can be given to drivers of the expected times traffic build up will occur, thus suggesting when drivers should make their trip, such as leaving before after after certain times to avoid rush hour.
		- Crash Management - the first responders arriving at a crash site - ambulance and fire fighters, paramedics, tow trucks, police, etc., are affected depending on the time of the day. During high traffic volume and rush hour times, responding to a crash around 4 or 5 PM has a vast difference between responding to a crash that takes place during midnight, or from 2 to 3 AM in the morning in terms of how fast and efficiently help can arrive. Accounting for PHV and DHV is important for preventing delays.
	- **Vehicle Mix (Vehicle Design** - The different types of vehicles (often including non motorized vehicles like bicycles) that make up the volume of a given road. This also includes form of public transit like shuttles, passenger vans, and buses, along with freight trucks when accounted with passenger sedans, SUVs and light trucks, pickup trucks, and motorcycles. Despite non motorized vehicles being seen as 'vehicles', they are often included in road design, though inefficiently with simple painted bike lanes. In fact, some traffic engineers and urban planners believe that non-motorized vehicles and motorized ones should be entirely separated 
	- **Level of Service (LOS)** - Graded scale that prioritizes drivers' levels of experience when driving on roads and intersections. Often, it discounts the experience of non-drivers and prioritizes road design choices that favor those behind the wheel, even if said design(s) are counter intuitive to actual road safety and efficiency in transportation time delays compared to alternative means; access to destinations and places of business, despite ideals, are also affected, such as the lack of foot traffic on areas with high LOS (like stroads), as, obviously, they are areas where pedestrians don't want to be in, and, as such, lack accessible areas of commerce, like side small stores and businesses, that aren't big box stores such as street shops found in urban areas. The scale of the LOS ranges from A to F in terms of how free flowing traffic is, with A through C representing Free flowing states, D a moderate amount of flow, and E and F slow paced traffic and gridlocked. Despite free flowing states being seen as optimal for drivers, they often impede proper use of land, encourages unsafe design (like higher speeds), and are prioritized over more pertinent factors for a an area, like housing, local places of business, and the general character of the area in which they are built. Often, LOS discounts:
		- Transit users and alternative forms of transit - buses, trains/metro/lights rail, trams, cyclists, and pedestrians
		- The actual temporal duration of traffic congestion (even with PHV)
		- A false correlation with congestion and delayed commute time and the economic viability of an area, often that even if congested, busy areas can still be economically viable (and are often indicative of being so).
		- The economic logistics of building roads and sprawled areas that adhere to free flow, along with the high costs of maintaining them.
		- High LOS, despite prioritizing driver experience, are associated with safety risks, air pollution, environment loss of habitat and trees, [[Heat Island Effect]], and housing displacement which includes initialized and systemic discrimination of minorities. 
		- Results in [[Induced Demand]]
	  ![[level-of-service-chart-source-Utah-DOT-1.webp]]
		It's important to note  that LOS is not a mandated law. Transportation agencies and municipalities are not required to use LOS and states can even use their own set of design standards or alternatives to LOS. Still, with the influence of the American Association of State and Highway and Transportation Officials (AASHTO) and the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA)'s design standards, specifically the Green Book and Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) specifically, states habitually use them as LOS is incorporated in these manuals; this is even so with official statements from the FHWA and AASHTO stating that there are no minimum requirements of the LOS that must be met, specially if they are even unattainable in some circumstances. 

**Environmental and Topographical Factors**
As based on the principles of civic engineering, the physical and natural conditions of the environment must be accounted for when designing a road.
-  **Terrain** - The type of terrain is important to consider when designing a road, especially with the climate and weather the terrain typically experiences; especially for how much water is expected to be present on the road. Such terrains to consider are:
	- Plains and grasslands
	- Mountainous regions
	- Plateaus
	- Hills and hillocks
	- Valleys and depressions
	- Canyons
	- Deserts 
	- Boreal Forests/Taiga 
	- Jungles
	- Swamps and wetlands
	- Karsts (i.e., rocky topographies)
- **Geotechnical and Soil Properties**
  Before anything can be built, the nature of the ground must be assessed, specifically the composition and state of the soil, rocks, hydrology, and general characteristics of the land (it's geology). This requires geotechnical engineering, which is a branch of civil engineering specifically studying how the materials that make up the earth of a project will respond to loads placed on them, such as buildings, bridges, and roads. Soil analysis is paramount, as it provides a literal foundation to prevent any form of collapse, costly repair and delays in construction. As such, several aspects of the soil needs to be examined, including
	- Soil composition and stability
	- Groundwater levels and drainage patterns
	- Bearing capacity (how much weight the soil can support)
	- Settlement potential (how much the ground might shift over time)
	- Seismic activity and earthquake risks
	- Slope stability and erosion potential
- **Drainage and Hydrology**
  Hydrology (the study of water and it's movement in an environment both above and below ground) is essential in civil engineering design as rainwater and other other sources (rivers, groundwater, runoff, evaporation) needs to be considered so that the project does not succumb to issues upon exposure, like structural damage, flooding, erosion, mold/corrosion, project delays, safety hazards. As such, engineering solutions for proper drainage and runoff in road design need to be considered, 
	- The rainfall a particular area receives
	- Water flow patterns as well as water velocity
	- The location and path for runoff during and after a storm
	- What soil is available and how quickly does water permeate through the soil
	- How high might rivers, ponds, and other bodies of water may rise during heavy rainfall or floods
	- The amount of water a drainage system can actually handle.
	- Flood levels including flood frequency, magnitude, and water surface elevation(s)
	- Flood plain boundaries
	- Scour potential (i.e., the erosion of soil and rock around the base of a structure (like abridge) from excess water)
	- Changes in water levels and quality based on seasonal variations.
	- Watershed characteristics
  
> <span style = "color: #008B8B;">All of these factors require accurate water data to infer so that structures, like roads, are stable and can survive the elements </span>

 Drainage and hydrology are important for preventing flooding, erosion and other problems caused by the elements, which can result in improved public safety, environmental sustainability, and lower the cost of repairs, delays, and maintenance. One of the most pivotal aspects of this is stormwater management, which deals with runoff. Runoff (surface runoff) is water that has accumulated in an environment but has nowhere to go, as concrete, asphalt, and building surfaces cannot absorb the water (unlike natural soil). If this runoff is not managed, it can lead to flooding and property damage. As such, engineers need to know expected rainfall volume and where sources of water can come from, as well as peak runoff rates, that goes into the planning of stormwater storage design and requirements as well as those for a drainage system. as solutions to runoff, engineers commonly use the following approaches:
 
- Storm drains
- Culverts (i.e., a conduit or channel (tunnel) built as a subterranean waterway. They are typically built under tunnels, bridges, roads, walkways, and natural formations (like the bottom edge of a hill or landmass.))
- Detention ponds
- Retention basins 
- Infiltration systems
- Swales (an exposed open air water channel lying within a grassy depression at the edge of a road) and channels
- Flood protection systems
- Drainage networks

In terms of New urbanism, there are sustainable approaches to water and storm management that can be implemented. These approaches, rather than move or redirect the runoff, focuses on instead observing it. This is mainly done by incorporating greenspaces that have natural grass and soil. When heavy rainfall or excess water enters theses spaces, rather than the water pooling and having be drained somewhere else, they are naturally absorbed within the soil, thus reducing the need for expensive and high maintenance storm drains or other water infrastructure. Additionally, greenspaces can also reduce the  [[Heat Island Effect]]. In away, these green spaces closely follow natural water cycles found within the environment. These approaches include:

**Climate and weather conditions**
- Temperature variations - Roads are impacted by daily weather, specifically if the the area is averagely hot or cold. Hot temperatures and heatwaves results in the pavement softening, causing it to expand. This can create rutting and potholes, especially on roads that receive high levels of traffic, in addition to stressing joints, like those on a bridge. In addition, during building or repair, high heat can limit construction, leading to costly delays.
- Freeze-Thaw Cycles - When cold weather brings snow and ice, the roads enter a freeze-thaw cycle. the water from freezing rain or snowmelt seeps into the road's pavement via cracks and pores. Upon freezing when temperatures drop below zero, the water expands as it turns into ice. This ice exerts pressure on the pavement and results in cracking. Over time, constant freezing and thawing of the roads damage it, resulting in surface deterioration and and potholes.  Added snow/ice also result in increased weight on the road, leading to potential structural problems due to overbearing loads. There is also the potential for the underlayers of the road (i.e., subgrade) to become affected by the freezing ground, casing shifts and heaves that result in uneven road surfaces. With cold weather, like heavy rainfall, driving conditions become hazardous, especially with icy conditions and the presence of black ice. Even when deicing salts and chemicals are used to melt the snow and ice, they can still cause damage to the road from corrosion and chemical breakdown which can further more seep into the environment. 
- Rainfall levels - Heavy rains can result in flooding, which can stress storm water management systems and overflood runoff infrastructure like culverts, detention ponds, and storm drains. Heavy rainfall can also lead to issues with erosion. Vehicles are more likely to crash during heavy rains, especially with the risk of hydroplaning and reduced visibility.

**Pavement Design Variables**
Determines the thickness and the layers of the road surface, be it asphalt or concrete. 
- Equivalent Single Axel Load (ESAL) - Related to a vehicles axel (i.e., it's wheels) and it's axels rotation plus it's weight. Essentially, it is a metric used to assess the loading a road can handle related to how often weighted car wheels drive upon it. It can also be seen as the damage a vehicle could make when taking into account it's load and the number of passes (spins) of it's wheel axels. Roads are designed to handle various ESALs with different mixed traffic bearing loads. 
- Material Properties
	- Layer coefficient(s) - In simple terms, the thickness of the pavement as based on an empirical number. this number takes into account how the thickness of the pavement will handle numerous factors, including
		- Traffic loading
		- Growths in truck numbers and similar heavy vehicles, 
		- Subgrade strength
		- Drainage
		- Type of pavement
		- The material properties of the pavement (i.e. impermeable vs semi permeable, how it can withstand temperature, how it expands, etc.) 
		- Reliability 
		- Design methods used by the firm or agency constructing the road
- Design Life - The expected lifespan of the road, taking into account the loads and elements that it can handle, before it needs to be maintained/renovated. Rather than a shelf life for the road or the presumption that it's not going to need repairs, the design life is more so an assessment of who preventative maintenance or reconstruction is going to be in the future. Typical design lives are set at 10, 20, or even 30 years. Design lives are not only implemented for new projects, but for existing projects as well, especially if they could use preventative measures for decay via refurbishment. 

**Human factors, safety, and Context**
- Not over relying on speed and traffic prediction models
- Not generalizing crashes with equal severity (for example, categorizing a fender bender the same as a head on collision)
- Not generalizing road users as being 'equal' in terms of their presence on the road (such as classifying bicycles in the same class as cars or pedestrians as equal in terms of the area they occupy compared to cars, especially large SUV's and pickup trucks)
- **Driver Psychology and Expectations**
	- Cultural factors - i.e., driving seen as a rite of passage and an expectant personal reasonability, even though it is technically optional, yet still socially driven and often compared to owning a house.
	- Marketing and it's psychological effects - portraying cars a manifestation of 'freedom', even though there is contrary evidence: financial burdens, environment and habitat destruction, gridlocked traffic, pretextual traffic stops, etc.
	- Type 1 and 2 thinking (thinking, fast and slow by Daniel Kahneman) and how drivers behave when they concentrate on a busy or complex road vs when they feel they're given leniency on a road that allows them to be fast.
	- Decision making and how flight or flight, emotional state, physical condition, and even brain chemistry (like inhibition of prefrontal cortex decision making due to over activity from the amygdala) can lead to rash and dangerous decisions.
	- Road rage and crimes related to road rage, including gun violence (especially in the US) and murder.
- **Multi-Modal Integration and alternate transit**
  Incorporating infrastructure -actual facilitating infrastructure and not mere after thoughts that alter the environment to allow for better accommodation and mobility - for those outside of a car. This includes proper sidewalks separated from traffic by bollards or trees; separated bike from cars (and ideally pedestrian sidewalks) that have physical barriers from cars (again, bollards, traffic walls, or tree lines) and not just painted lanes that are even singled on main roads; traffic lights, curbs with detectable warning surfaces, and similar infrastructure that complies with Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) standards; raised pedestrian islands; less wide lanes  and shorter crossing distances in areas where pedestrians are expected to be; dedicated traffic signaling that counts for pedestrians upon button press; prohibiting turns on red when pedestrians are present; incorporation of other forms of transit with prominent frequency (i.e., multiple transit units taking the same route and arriving at different transit stops frequently instead of one or two units: that have long waiting windows and limited stops) including; busses with dedicated reserved single lanes; railed forms of transit like passenger train, metro, high speed rail, and light rail; trams; cycling paths; and areas that promote walking paths and walkability in general. 
- **Right-of-Way (ROW) and Land Use**
  Revising Right-of-way practices laws to be more prioritized (or at least less exclusive) for pedestrians and individuals outside of a car than drivers - especially considering that even when crossing at designated intersections and crosswalks, pedestrians are still blamed for being involved the collision; being more stringent on drivers who are involved in pedestrian crashes and not blaming the pedestrian for being present; prohibiting turns on red when pedestrians are present or when trying to cross; outlaw and repeal jaywalking laws as they are biased towards drivers and against racial minorities. In terms of land use, restrict single zoning and allow for mixed used laws that help facilitate middle housing and buildings; prohibit parking minimums and instead advocate for maximums without arbitrary rules to establish maximums; and reforming land acquisition practices and costs.
