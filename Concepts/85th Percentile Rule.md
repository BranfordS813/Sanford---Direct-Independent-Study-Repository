
The 85th Percentile rule is a road design concept which determines the speed limit for a car on a given road. It is more so based on a population estimate gathered by observing how a majority of drivers drive along the road at a certain speed. Basically, the speed limit is set depending on how drivers, on average, drive at a certain speeds, but, the limit is adjusted to give a window (typically 5 to 10 miles) for driving at a faster speed, with 15 percent of the drives who drive even faster speed discounted.

For instance, suppose when observing drivers driving down a stretch of road, 85 percent of the drivers drive at an average of 35 miles per hour, but some of them may drive at 40 or even 50 miles per hour, while a rare few drive at, say, 70 o 80 miles per hour - i.e., the majority of the 15 percent; let's say 10 percent of drivers drive at a slightly faster speed of 40-50 mph while 5 percent drive faster. To accommodate for the people who may drive at 40 or 50 miles per hour, but not for the people who driver at faster speeds, rather than setting the speed limit based on the average driving speed of 35 miles per hour, the speed limit is increased to a window of around 5 to 10 miles, such as a 45 mile per hours speed limit. 

Despite the 85th Percentile rule being seen as 'essential' and a necessity in traffic engineering, it has flaws. For one, it was originally designed for rural roads. It's incorporation into other road types, including stroads, has lead to increases in crashes and pedestrian collisions, though, traffic engineers still rely on it, believing that faster speeds can accommodate drivers ability to reach destinations faster and more efficiently and, although counter intuitive, 'safely' via reducing the risk for collisions if all vehicles are traveling at the same speed rather than some traveling slow or fast amongst each other. But, as based on more other studies, raising the speed based on the 85th percentile only increases crashes, and the time difference saved from increased speeds is negligible by a few seconds (like 40 seconds) and is in no way tied to a return on productivity when reaching a destination faster.
