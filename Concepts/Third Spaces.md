Within one's everyday, the spaces that they occupy can be categorized in two major areas - home and work. Home serves as the ultimate space one is familiar with, as they live, sleep, and experience the personal lives within this space. The second space is work or school, where the individual either is instructed and undergoes their education within a school setting, or they make their living. As such, this space, although secondary to home in terms of time prevalence, is a significant factor within an individuals life. Within current America, this dichotomy of spaces - home and work/school - is what makes up the majority of where a person develops their life outside of commute and commerce. Still, there exists a palace that lies in between home and work. Often, in more walkable and dense urban areas, there are places that can be easily accessed when one wants to spend time between home and work, especially at the expense of one's leisure. These places are typically outside the first and second place (home and work) where one can take comfort to chat and connect with other members of their community. Third spaces, by their nature, are communal and encourage socialization, in turn leading to better social cohesion and less division, especially political and ideological division that is typically seen in isolated communities separated by sprawl. It is important though to distinguish third spaces from loitering. Loitering is being present within a public space for a prolonged period of time without any reason or purpose, typically seen as anticipating to commit an actual act against the law like prostitution (in areas where prostitution is illegal), drug dealing, robbery, etc. Partaking in third spaces vasty differs from loitering as they are places that people go to with an intended purpose and further where people are expected to act in a certain way, whether that is to socialize, relax, study, etc. In fact, most third spaces based on commerce have a 'hidden rule' where, although not explicitly enforced, is expected to be followed, like purchasing a food item from a cafe or eatery, or buying a drink or a dish at a bar; however, there are third spaces that are indented to be fully free for public use without any need to purchase anything, such as parks and public libraries. Third places are open to public and are meant for people to occupy in with their intended purpose. But more importantly, one has no obligation to go into a third space, stay in a third space, or even pay to be in a third space. Third spaces, in effect, act as hubs were members of a community can interact, even if they're strangers - a meeting ground for the purpose of building relationships that are not at home or in work. One paramount effect of third spaces involved how individuals not only develop and affirm their own identities, but to acknowledge and understand the identities of others, thus helping to build empathy and bridge gaps between different cultures, life styles, and identities. Such interactions broaden individual's sense of identity and allows them to view them selves as part of a larger society. 

Despite the benefits of third spaces, accessing them in most American towns and areas faces challenges due to car-centricity, such as lack of public transit as well as elongated travel times via car due to rush hours and heavy traffic flow. This is complicated further as third spaces within sprawled areas are deigned to be far from residential areas well beyond walking distance where they require a car. Further, some third space are simply not available, like libraries or malls and similar shopping centers. For most sprawled areas, in order to access third spaces, and to that extent participate in society, one needs to own a car, and that's even if one is willing to deal with potential delays in traffic. As such, most people in sprawled areas typically reside either at home or work between shopping and other direct tasks to be performed without relaxing, social interaction, and winding down at one place. 

Due to a lack of easy access to third spaces, cultural ideologies that could prove problematic in the long one have arose, of which a key one is individualism (and in some cases, hyper individualism). Due to lack of third spaces, people typically spend more time at home. Without interacting with others with different backgrounds and experiences, people then to develop individualistic mindsets rather than collective and thus will not want to connect with others in their community. Such emphasis on individualism can lead to feelings of isolation which can have impacts on mental health. And for second spaces (work), the lack of wanting to go to a third spaces leads to more time dedicated to working. This can encourage productivity, but more so within a capitalist frame work where labor is prioritized of leisure time and building relationships with friends, family, and members within a community. Ironically, some may be go to extreme lengths to transform their homes to mimic third spaces, such as converting parts of their house into a home theater; building a personal bowling alley; building personal game rooms with billiards, darts, arcade cabinets, etc.; home coffee stations with furniture stylized after bistro seats; built in bars with beer on tap; personal libraries (expansive libraries, not just a large collection of books on a shelf; large scale aesthetic decor such as mounted signs, neon signs, and displays; personal gyms and sports areas like indoor basketball and tennis courts. Still, despite these attempts, a home cannot be converted into a third space, because a home lacks the ability to allow to meet new people and connect to others with perspective and experiences different from our own, especially if people may not even be willing to make such connections when isolated and confined within their homes. A community can only be created outside of the home, while online communities are simply not equivalent as they can further isolate people and serve as echo chambers for their pre-established ideologies.

Types of third spaces can be categorized into four areas: Free and Public Spaces; Food and Drink Establishments; Recreation and Wellness centers; and Specialty and Cultural Hubs. Above all, there are exists a set of conditions that define and characterizes a third space:

**Characteristics of a third space:**
- They are places of neutral ground which promote engagement and civic life
- They promote a sense of connection with the community
- They can act as places for networking and give opportunities to increase social capital
- People may attend them as groups (though others may frequent them individually) 
- Conversation with one's company is the main focus
- Everybody has equal footing and importance, there is no one leading the group besides occasionally leading the conversation 
- The place is accessible and accommodating
- The place keeps a low profile. It is not glaring and conspicuous but is rather typically quiet and nestled
- The place usually promotes a light and relax atmosphere. People are often in a happy or playful mood.
- The place acts, in a sense, as a 'home away from home'.

Although car-centricity - with it's effects on societal atomization and overreliance on automotive transit causing traffic that people do not want to otherwise want to unnecessarily deal with deal with - there are other factors to consider with the decline of third spaces within the US. Such factors can be attributed to: higher costs of living, resulting in people working more hours and even attaining multiple jobs that demand time (i.e., Hustle Culture and the Gig Economy); people with demanding lives that require errands and chores and even caring responsibilities that make going to third spaces difficult; the rise of social media and virtual social spaces like forum chats, Reddit, social media networks, and discord servers; the rise of online shopping with fast paced delivery and the decline of brick and mortar stores - this also goes for food delivery services like Uber Eats and DoorDash; the rise streaming services and the ability to stream media on multiple devices (phone, tablet, laptop/desktop) with services like Netflix, Hulu, Peacock, PlutoTV, Paramount+, Disney+, Crunchyroll, etc. - this also goes for gaming platforms that feature digital transactions and online multiplayer/MMO play ; rises in anti-loitering laws; cultural complications such as racial and political divide and racial bias to target minorities of wrongdoing, such as loitering; decline in religious attendance; underfunding of public areas like libraries, community centers, and public parks via budget cuts and limited operation hours; declining interest in community based clubs and hobbies; and, ironically, work-from-home, where, despite there not being a physical second place (work), the is melding of home and work that otherwise, rather than encourage people to seek out places to convene outside of work, encourages them to stay at home and isolate themselves, especially as a result of burnout from workloads. There is also the fact that third spaces, for the most part, sometimes involve spending money or at least spending money to have a good reason to be there. As such, people may not have the funds to spend time nor money at third spaces, further complicated if going to third spaces takes time and money in and of itself, such as dealing with traffic and paying for fuel or even public transit.

Still, third spaces are important for building community and promoting mental health. They also important to allow people to connect with each other and willing to have conversations they wouldn't otherwise have within isolation. Despite the setbacks that third spaces receive in modern times, they can still be adapted where rather than being destinations in themselves, they can more so serve as areas where people can passively stay, linger, and even make conversation with others. And, again addressing setbacks third spaces face, the Covid-19 pandemic clearly highlighted their need, as being barred from them otherwise increase social isolation and the negative effects of mental health that came with it, notably, rises in depression and anxiety. At the very least, compromises can be made, such as working not at home, but in a 3rd space, like a library or coffee shop or even a public park; however, it is still important to connect with others within theses spaces (though, social media can be utilized to some extent when working or staying within a third space). 

This is not to say that third spaces are endangered and will face being wiped out. They will stay in some form, especially given that humans are social creatures by nature. Still, third spaces can be heavily affected by social and economic factors beyond car-centricity or services that affect them, especially their availability and accessibility within certain areas. For instance, third spaces may be rather common in economic or financially viable areas whereupon the upper middle class may reside (or even the middle class). But, third spaces may be few and far between within areas with high poverty and minority populations. Hence, third spaces should not only be preserved and given importance, but they should be made more accessible and equitable for all demographics.

Third places, in essence, are a heaven. They're not inherent places to deliberately perform actions with an agenda or make calculated plans, like those typically done at home or work. They're to offer respite and an opportunity to unwind and open up and build report with others to foster the sense of a community. Although they may not totally disappear, they are still threatened with mass disuse. People do not have to spend hours within these third spaces, but any amount of time, even small, can prove beneficial. And even, at times, life changing for the better. This especially follows for the effects that third spaces have on children, as they can help them gain a better sense of self identity, their community, and to enrich their life with diversity and a greater appreciation for the world around them. Third places were even found to be associated with academic benefits, including lower rates of drop out, higher test scores and GPAs, and homework being routinely completed. Third places also help children improve their overall behavior and allows them to learn how to properly regulate their emotions and promote self control and the ability to build better relations with their peers. All of this can help children develop skills related to soft skills and emotional intelligence, specifically involving problem-solving, conflict resolution, developing leadership skills, and proper discernment (decision making). Still, as previously stated with the current relationship with third space availability in impoverished areas, there also lies a potential issue with third spaces in that the quality of the third space can also vary. Third spaces that have high quality programs with proper resources and provisions as well as good staff relationships with students or patrons can be very beneficial, and can have a heavily influence of behavior and academic impacts with students. still, if they are of low quality, they may be either inefficient, or, in worst cases, do aggravate problems. At the very least, though, third places should be seen a safe area to explore and enrich oneself within the community around them.

For most people living in car-centric and sprawled areas, like suburbs and exurbs, third spaces, even if few, do exists. The issues is that they are inaccessible due to the sprawled natured of car centered infrastructure where they can only be reached by a car. This is especially problematic for those who do not drive, be it for age (old/young), disability, financial circumstance, or even simple life choice. But with youth especially, the lack of a car only leads to isolation. Even within a neighborhood that is 'technically' walkable via neighborhood blocks, theses areas a separated from more communal spaces. The only way to go to such spaces is with a car. In terms of surface area, one factor of car-centricity that heavily lead to the decline of third spaces were parking minimums. With minimums, and arbitrary rules based on them such as a certain amount of square footage requiring a full sized parking space, mass areas of land are paved and used for parking, even to the point where the parking area is larger than the building itself, such as commonly found with big box stores. All of the land used for parking could instead provide real estate for much beneficial purposes towards the community, including third spaces, along with other collective goods like parks, public transit lanes, community spaces, schools, etc. Another contributing factor are zoning laws, especially single family zoning laws (typically responsible for sprawled suburbs) which prohibit mixed use areas or places of business or commerce within a residential neighborhood.
