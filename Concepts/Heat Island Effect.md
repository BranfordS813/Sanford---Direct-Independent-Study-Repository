
The Heat Island Effect is a climate related phenomenon that involves excessive heat caused by the sun, further complicated by entrapment from green house gases, and the pavement/asphalt of an environment, specifically those found in sprawled suburban areas, car-centric areas with expanding roads, and especially in expansive parking lots. During hot and humid days, heat is emitted onto the pavement. In natural, excessive heat is countered via transpiration, a process where water travels up the roots of a plant and turns into water vapor when emitted via small orifices on the underside of a leaves, essentially cooling the air. Shading provided by plants and trees also helps reduce temperatures. But, within areas with large amounts of concrete and asphalt, the excess heat is absorbed onto the surface, especially as a result of the dark colors commonly found in such materials, like black, brown, or grey (specifically darker shades of grey). At night, the heat is released, but increases temperatures while doing so. And due to the impervious nature of paved surfaces, there is no way for water to penetrate and evaporate to cool the air like that found in plants. Overtime, with constant releases of heat, the area becomes hotter, creating a 'heat island'. Areas with car-centric infrastructure and expanded pavement are typically hotter than those without, like parts of rural areas or even urban areas with portions of green spaces and walkable places, like parks. While heat islands found in sprawled areas are problematic, heat islands found in urban areas are also troublesome, but, as mentioned with previous countermeasures like parks and more green spaces, urban areas also attempt to incorporate building materials that can aid in cooling - permeable materials - that allow water to flow though rather than pooled and drained. Another counteractive measure is to 'lighten' streets by coating surfaces with light colored reflective coating, such as light grey, to better reflect excess heat. Yet another solution is to place gardens or even grassy lawns on top of roof tops to make use of plant transpiration.

In all, the heat island effect is a phenomenon which does not discriminate on the anthropomorphic environment it affects.  There is however debate on whether it affects sprawled vs urban areas more. With dense urban areas, high rises and sky scrapers along with paved roads and streets can be major contributors; however, sprawled areas can be just as consequential if not more so, especially counting for larger surface areas of pavement that include stretches of road and expansive parking lots with virtually no shade. Vehicle and fossil fuel emissions of course complicate the problem.

- Kidney disease
- Mental Illness
