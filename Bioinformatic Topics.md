
### What is Bioinformatics?
Related to genetics and genomics, bioinformatics is the practice of storing, collecting, analyzing, and disseminating biological data and information. this includes genetic data; DNA sequences (FASTA and FASTQ); proteomics; gene interactions and pathways (such as the Kegg Database); genetic annotations; biomarkers. Bioinformatics organizes and indexes this information for the purposes of gaining insights into health,  disease, for medical care such as targeted medicine and drug discovery. 

Bioinformatics pairs biological research with data informatics. While gaining access and collecting information in the past proved difficult, it is now facile. As such, the goal of bioinformatics is to understand and interpret this collected information. Computer based and data analytic methods are the default approach for this.

#### Potential Topics

- **Cancers**
  Work can be done to find biomarkers or targeted/personalized medicine related to mutations (somatic mutations), tumor heterogeneity, and gene expression 
  <br>
- **Cardiovascular disease** 
  Identifying genetic risks typically using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to identify genetic risks and variants that are linked and can cause heart conditions
  <br>
-  **Neuropsychiatric disease**
   Identifying genetic risk factors that lead to mental issues and brain disorders or polygenic risks of conditions like schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease
   <br>
- **Rare and inherited disorders**
  Mendelian and rare diseases: detecting genetic genes that can cause diseases when expressed. typically done with exome or whole genome sequencing pipelines (with tools like Exomiser). Once sequenced, tools are used to search de novo pathogenic variants of genes which cause diseases.
  <br>
- **Mitochondrial disorders** 
  Genetic diseases related to mitochondria, using pipelines related to DNA variations, heteroplasmy, and haplogroups.
  <br>
-  **Genetic epidemiology and statistical genetics**
   Utilizing statistical genetics - drawing inferences from genetic data via mathematical and statistical models; and epidemiology - studying how diseases and conditions originate and distribute among a population; as well as patterns and determinants of disease conditions within populations, are both used to study and identify<span style = "color: blue;"> genetic risk factors </span>for complex clinical traits and discovering genes related to diseases and clinical outcomes. Studies help predict who may be at an increased risk for certain diseases. Methods can be examined for determining risk factors for disease via linking inherited variation (i.e., the difference in DNA traits passed down from parents to offspring)

- **Pleiotropic genetic effects**
  Pleiotropy are conditions which a change or mutation within a single gene, or a variant of a gene, can result in changes which influence phenotypic traits, (i.e., a pleiotropic gene). Sometimes, multiple phenotypic traits are simultaneously occur due to multiple cells being affected and sharing signaling pathways. 
  <br>
- **Polygenic risk prediction**
  Determining the risk an individual may be susceptible to acquiring a genetic disorder, multi-gene traits, or a disease. This is done via observing the effects of genetic variants in a large number. For example, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP), but more so done as an aggregate via small effects over several DNA variants. Such prediction is usually performed as a Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) which measures an individual's inherited genetic predisposition for certain conditions such as heart disease or cancers. Still, this score is calculated as a risk comparison to the general population
  <br> 
- **Functional Genomics**
  The practice of annotating genomes to gain a better understanding of genetic mechanisms and functions. This can lead to better insight and interpretation of genetic genomic variations for individuals in addition to expanding knowledge of various diseases and cancers. It can also be important for discoveries in biomedicine and individualized medicine. A challenge, though, is that theses variations are within non-coding regions of DNA, making them difficult to detect and understand. <span style="color: blue;"> Variant discover is hence an important field </span>, especially for finding epigenomic alterations of genes across the entire genome. Gene expression with epigenomic influences and causes are mapped for several diseases like cancers, tissues diseases and disorders at the cell level. <span style = "color: blue;"> (i.e., epigenome maps) </span>Note that this also deals with chromatin and RNA sequencing. Investigating epigenome maps and how genes are expressed typically via single cell analysis) can help decipher how non-coding regions of cells function. This can give insights into how diseases develop and progress.
  <br>
- **Pharmacogenomics**
  Statistical genetics and epidemiology applied to reveal variations in DNA/gene expression which can help identify how people may respond to different medications. Pharmacogenomics is specifically the study of how genetics may respond to drugs - an essential requirement for understanding the mechanics and effectiveness of drugs, how treatment outcomes can be better predicted, and for the purpose of developing personalized medicine.

- **Single cell genomics**
  Focuses on single cell sequencing, which can offer a more precise insight into the mechanism of a disease, such as studies done with methylome and transcriptome. Topics include: differential abundance, expression analysis, multi-omics integration, and<span style = "color:blue;"> predicvtive modeling. </span> 
  <br>
-  **Proteomics and metabolomics**
   The proteome and metabolome are what links an organism's genotype to it's phenotype. They are also involved in how an organism responds to external stimuli. More specifically, the subject(s) delve into the study of protein's, metabolites, and their interactions. Tools are often used to: measure structure of proteins; modification of proteins; how proteins are expressed; the number of metabolites present in the system at any given time; metabolic pathways. When applied to computational biology, proteomic and metabolomic data is used to derive differences in how proteins and metabolites are expressed or modified, typically studied between an experimental condition or a patient group vs a control group. The two can also be used to study diseases such as clinical diagnostics, cancer phenomics (i.e., the study of an organisms phenotype and physical traits (include biochemical traits) and how they change over time due to genes and the environment, in this case with cancer in terms of how genes lead to physical changes in cell shapes, behaviors, interactions with tissues, and metabolic outputs.)
   <br>
- **Digital pathology**
  Involves the use whole slide images, but taken at extremely high depths of detail and resolution, commonly resulting in large quantities of data. These dense and high quality images can then be used for a number of purposes, like accurate quantification of structures, examining cell processes, somatic mutation, copy number variants, structural variants vs classification, segmentation, and localization. Whole slide images can be converted into more accessible and easier to read memory digital files with reduced file size for digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format. IDCOM can be used for a variety of purposes, like
	- Classification of diseases (tumor vs normal cells/tissues) 
	- Nuclear detection and segmentation 
	- Digital ink removal
	- Anomaly detection 
Whole slide imaging can be applied to artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches
