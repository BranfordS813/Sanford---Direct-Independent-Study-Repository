- **Neighborhood 5-Minute Walk**
  Building compact neighborhoods (similar to 15-Minute Cities)
  <br>
- **The Missing Middle**
  Most housing either falls within the range of single family housing and large scale apartment complexes. Often, different forms of housing are missing within the American landscape, specially in suburbia and rural areas. This includes
	- Town Houses
	- Cottage Courts
	- Duplexes
	- Quadruplexes 
	- Live-works
	- Courtyard complexes 
	- Small apartment buildings
	- Granny flats 
  The missing middle is important because it incorporates density without needing large or sprawled infrastructure, like suburbs or large apartment complexes 
  <br>
- **Rethinking parking policies**
  Parking lots carry a lot of problems, from environmental impacts (such a the [[Heat Island Effect]] and climate change) to the cost of maintaining them. But, one of the biggest issues is that of the large expanse of space parking lots require, which results in bulldozing previous neighborhoods or destroying natural habitats to sustain them. 
  <br>
- **Incremental development**
  Developing livable places within small increments, instead of mass development. Furthermore, areas are incrementally built by individuals and small scale developers, not massive corporations. 
  <br>
- **Katrina Cottage(s)**
  In response to hurricane Katrina, several small cottages (small housing unites) were constructed to house those affected by the hurricane. These units are small enough to not use up too much land use. they can further be densified, yet provide comfortable living space. They are sturdy, well accommodating, and are still in use today. 
  <br>
- **Doing the math (considering financial logistics of urban vs rural/sprawled areas)**
  Overall, it costs more to maintain sprawled areas and the infrastructure associated with them. This includes stroads and big box stores, especially more so than urban areas. There are also lower property taxes, city retail taxes, low foot traffic for potential business, and lower jobs for rural/sprawled areas compared to more densified cities and towns. |
  <br>
- **Tactical urbanism**
  Essentially a soft form of infrastructure vigilantism, although some legitimate city organizers and planners are involved where ideas and projects are tested with small but effective changes made within environments to increase mobility, densification, livability, and alternate forms of transit besides cars - all performed by concerned citizens and parties.  This is done often (though not always as some legitimate organizations and bureaucratic processes can be involved) without notification by city/town municipalities or DOTs. Examples include temporary bicycle lane painting, traffic calming techniques like improvised bollards (i.e., potted plants), signage, converting parking lots into pop-up parks (i.e., parklets) and promoting improvised places to eat and socialize. 
  <br>
- **Mixed urban use/centers**
  In sprawled and rural areas, places of housing, businesses, commerce and shopping, and office/work are often deliberately separated, like shopping centers centers/districts/malls, and office parks. Mixed urban use allows all forms of utilitarian and commercial places of business, commercial use, and work to be located directly next or within livable areas with housing. For example - a corner store near a home (typically a home or duplex converted into one); a small business like a backyard farm or a small paper mill within a neighborhood; offices that are within walkable distance of homes. All of these are examples of mixed urban use and mixed urban centers.
  <br>
- **Suburban retrofitting**
  Similar to mixed urban use. Basically, taking pre-existing suburbs (especially suburbs that have been built during the mid 20th century following the WW2 boom) and converting them into more walkable areas. More specifically, it deals with refurbishing areas in suburbia that are either abandoned or under used and converting them into walkable areas. This includes defunct shopping malls, office parks, parking lots, etc., as well as similar suburban areas that are commonly isolated. Like other New urbanism strategies, the goal is to reduce automotive dependency.
  <br>
- **Traditional neighborhood development (TOD)**

   Building new neighborhoods and town developments, but, not focused on sprawl or car dependency. Instead, as based on past building patterns prior to the advent of the car, they are built on the basis of being at a human scale with walkability and public transit in mind. 
   <br>
- **Architecture that the city puts first**
  Architecture in the modern age is often constructed to be attention grabbing and 'present' in a way to impose importance and grandiosity; this can be akin to treating architecture as an object to be admired, like a sculpture, or to impose a sense of importance or noticeability such as a sign or a large advertisement. But, architecture, when such emphasis is placed on presence, leaves little room for 'in-between' spaces where people can congregate. public outside places are just as important as within being in private or publicly owned inside places. So, instead of dedicating large masses of land to architecture or architectural features to only be looked at, they can instead be scaled down or even converted to public walkable and communal areas, like converting a facade or a garage into a public plaza for people to convene. 
  <br>
  - **Form-based Codes** 
   Rather than using conventional zoning laws that restrict land use to single use purposes like housing, commercial/commerce, and business, Form-based codes focus on reforming zoning laws to be less restrictive and more inclusive of shared public space, like mixed use neighborhoods, and different approaches to build neighborhoods, be it incremental or complete. 
   <br>
- **Lean Urbanism**
  Similar to Incremental Development. Rather than large corporate entities building sprawled neighborhoods en masse, lean urbanism focuses on community-driving approaches to development. This includes allowing individuals or communities to have an easier time forming development business, and provide more housing and development that is more easily attainable. All of this is for the purpose of creating a holistic community as opposed to relying on outside developers to build while maintaining all the power and decision making. lean urbanism gives individuals with limited capital to a say in how they want their communities to be built.
  <br>
- **Light Imprint/Green Infrastructure**
  Focused on reducing the amount of hard infrastructure and concrete built within a city/environment. This especially falls for suburban design, which relies heavily on concrete, pavement, and asphalt - all commonly used for wide roads and parking lots which are further complicated more by sprawl. As a result, rain and storm water/drain management is ca common issue, as well as expensive with infrastructure and engineering solutions for storm water drainage construed all the time. Hence, Light Imprint/Green Infrastructure amends this issue by reducing  or eliminating over reliance on paved surfaces and instead use actually soil and greenery This allows rain water to naturally collect and filter int the ground without the need for expensive water drainage systems.

- **Context based street design**
  Focuses on designing streets according tot heir actual use and function. Often, streets and roads are combined into 'stroads' that try to do two functions at once:
	- Act as a throughfare for cars as a road
	- Function as a street for people on foot to access different areas
  In reality, stroads fail at both, as their complex design, with multiple in and out access points/driveway aprons and intersections, impedes actual traffic flow; while discouraging those outside of a car from walking on them as the design proves to be very dangerous and life threatening from the high speeds and unsafe crossings of stroads. Further, stroads are often biased and accommodate cars over people. This differs from actual streets, which serves more as destinations than conduits for cars. Here, streets are focused on walkability at a human scale with areas and centers where people can commonly walk and commune as a community. Unlike stroads, streets allow people to safely navigate their environment and enter established business without the need for a car or at least with public transit. These establishments could be: corner stores; restaurants and food eateries; small business; municipal services; offices and medical suites; 3rd space, etc. - all accessible without the need for a car. 
  As such, unlike stroads, streets are shared community spaces. Even with cars incorporated, streets are designed (or should be designed) to prioritize people, having low speed limits with safe and accessible crossings. With this, context based street design effectively uncombines stroads and focuses on separating streets from roads, but more so on how the are is actually used as well as the activities that are done in that area. Rather than a one-form fits all approach, streets are designed based on actual usage, such as if people commonly walk, cycle, or travel by public transit rather than car. This also goes for how people may actually access and use places of business or commerce, etc. When comes to road design, there are numerous considerations to consider: [[Road Design Considerations]]. This also goes for cars as well, with considerations for targeted speeds (as opposed to speed limits and not over reliant on the [[85th Percentile Rule]]) and how often roads are actually used, especially in rural areas. Context based street design takes flexible approaches to designing new areas and refurbishing older areas or modifying existing streets with an emphasis on deciding whether the new plan or design will actually be supported in it's intended manner, rather than altering neighborhoods and towns only for the new development to be under used (or even overused). <span style = "color: #008B8B;">Question, is there public data on streets that save money vs streets that don't as based on their design?</span>
